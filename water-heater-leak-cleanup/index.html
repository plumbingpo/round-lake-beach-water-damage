Right Now

What to Do Right Now

  1. Cut the Power or GasElectric units get shut down at the breaker. For gas, turn the control knob to off. Running the burner or the element with no water in the tank is something to avoid every time.
  2. Close the Cold SupplyFind the cold water shutoff above the heater and turn it clockwise. If it is stuck or leaking, shut off the main water valve for the house.
  3. Beware of Scalding WaterWhat comes out of the tank can cause scalds. Pets and children should stay clear, and nobody should walk into pooled water before it cools down.
  4. Drain the Tank SafelyIf you can reach the drain valve, attach a garden hose and run it outside or to a floor drain so the remaining water does not empty onto the floor.
  5. Move Stored ItemsAnything absorbent, including boxes and rugs, should come up from the wet area. Fabric and cardboard absorb water fast, then keep it pressed to the concrete and walls.
Will Insurance Pay?

What About Insurance?

When a tank fails suddenly, the water damage to floors, walls and belongings is often covered as sudden and accidental. The water heater itself usually is not, since wearing out with age is considered maintenance. Because coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks, a claim may be denied or reduced when the adjuster sees indications the tank leaked for a long time, like old stains or trails of rust. Read your policy, take photos of the damage and the heater, and do not dispose of the old unit before the claim is settled.

Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • Drain pans beneath closet or attic units that rust through or clog and then spill over.
  • A missing expansion tank or a failed thermostat that lets too much pressure build inside.
  • Water discharged when the pressure and temperature relief valve fails or opens.
  • Fittings left slightly loose by recent repair or installation work.
  • Sediment and corrosion that eat through the bottom of an aging steel tank.
If It Is Left

What the Water Damages

Stand and Subfloor Damage

More water goes into the subfloor or wooden stand beneath the tank than anywhere else. Rot can weaken it, a real concern once a full new tank sits on top.

Stored Belongings

Whatever is kept by a closet or garage heater, from keepsakes to furniture and boxes, is the first thing to get wet. Cardboard falls apart, and wet contents can mold if they are not dried or discarded quickly.

Ceilings Under an Attic Heater

When an attic heater leaks, water passes through the insulation and lands on the ceiling underneath. Wet insulation stops insulating and its weight can bring ceiling drywall down.

Flooring in Nearby Rooms

Carpet, hardwood or laminate in the hallway gets wet as water escapes below the closet door. Heat makes it worse, with quicker swelling of wood boards and carpet backing that comes apart.
